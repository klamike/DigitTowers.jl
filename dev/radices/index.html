Other numeral systems

Decimal is only one place to look for a numeral that computes its own value. radix_sweep searches the same single-digit power grammar in any positional radix, retaining exact integers and external root orders.

using DigitTowers

original = radix_sweep(10, 2:100; roots=1:1000)
fixed = only(radix_sweep(22, 3:100; roots=[1]).solutions)
@assert fixed.number == big(15)^9 == 38443359375
@assert fixed.digits == [15, 9, 1, 10, 7, 2, 10, 3]
@assert verify_radix(fixed)

Digits above nine are integer values: a digit 15 means fifteen in both the positional numeral and the tower. roots=[1] removes the external root. There are no grouped blocks or consumed root digits in this API; use Config for those decimal grammars.

Three root-free discoveries

Searching radices 3–64, lengths 2–100, and roots 1–1000 gives 49 solutions from 14,284 numeral candidates, with the default forbidden 0^0. Only seven are root-free, and three of those have more than two digits:

RadixNumeralInteger valueEffective power
17C9D041455,159,780,352$12^9$
22F91A72A338,443,359,375$15^9$
34GTC0A31NJLBXC7V0UPJK9EG83,076,749,736,557,242,056,487,941,267,521,536$16^{29}$

Here A=10, B=11, …, X=33. The radix-22 example contains no zero. Its third digit is one, so the tail after that digit collapses:

\[[15,9,1,10,7,2,10,3]_{22} = 15^{9^{1^{10^{7^{2^{10^3}}}}}} = 15^9.\]

The other two examples use a zero whose positive exponent makes the next power equal to one. These are exact fixed points, with all digits used once in their original order; they need no square root.

The remaining root-free solutions are $[2,4]_6=16$, $[3,3]_8=27$, $[2,6]_{29}=64$, and $[4,4]_{63}=256$. Decimal contributes its original square-root solution $262144$ and no root-free solution in this search. These statements concern the stated finite bounds, not arbitrary lengths or radices.

Reproduce every row with:

julia --project research/radix_experiments.jl

The checked-in research/results/radix_solutions.tsv records digit values, root orders, and effective exponents; companion files record the search bounds and candidate counts. The research script verifies each result and repeats the restricted search below. Unit tests independently enumerate every small-radix numeral and evaluate short suffixes literally.

An infinite family you can prove

For every integer $a\ge3$, choose radix $\beta=a^{a-1}-1$. Then

\[[a,a]_\beta=a\beta+a=a^a.\]

Both digits fit the radix, so this produces infinitely many root-free tower numerals without zeros or ones:

$a$Radix $\beta$NumeralValue
38[3,3]27
463[4,4]256
5624[5,5]3,125
67,775[6,6]46,656

More generally, two-digit root-free examples satisfy $\beta=(a^b-b)/a$: choose $b$ divisible by $a$ and check that $\beta>\max(a,b)$. For instance, $a=2,b=4$ gives radix six and $[2,4]_6=2^4$. This is a construction rather than a bounded search claim.

Turn the radix into a root-order dial

The same integer can survive in a whole run of numeral systems. Write $A=a^{a-1}$ and choose any positive integer $r$ with $(a+1)r<A$. In radix $\beta=A-r$, the two-digit numeral is

\[[a,ar]_{A-r}=a(A-r)+ar=a^a =\sqrt[r]{a^{ar}}.\]

Thus increasing the radix by one decreases the required root order by one. For $a=4$, every radix 52–63 represents 256 as such a tower:

RadixDigitsRoot orderIdentity
52[4,48]12$\sqrt[12]{4^{48}}=256$
57[4,28]7$\sqrt[7]{4^{28}}=256$
62[4,8]2$\sqrt{4^8}=256$
63[4,4]1$4^4=256$

The root-free $[a,a]$ family is the $r=1$ endpoint of this construction. No search is needed to establish the whole family.

A complete result when zeros and ones are forbidden

In every radix 3–64, with external root order 1–1000, every solution whose digits are all at least two has exactly two digits. There are 38 such solutions in the atlas. Unlike the unrestricted atlas, this conclusion covers all lengths.

The finite part is exhaustive search over lengths two through four. The remaining lengths have a simple growth certificate. If the full tower is $T=d_1^E=N^r$ and every digit is at least two, then

\[E < rL\log_2\beta \le 6000L.\]

For five digits, the smallest possible suffix is $2^{2^{2^2}}=65536>6000\cdot5$. Increasing the length changes that minimum suffix from $m$ to $2^m$; its growth stays ahead of the linear bound. Thus length five and every longer length are impossible in this radix/root range. The familiar decimal example depends on its digit one, while the nondecimal root-free family avoids that dependency by using two digits.

# This finite search, together with the growth bound, covers every length.
for radix in 3:64
    result = radix_sweep(radix, 2:4; roots=1:1000,
                         allowed_digits=2:radix-1, max_solutions=10_000)
    @assert result.complete
    @assert all(x -> length(x.digits) == 2, result.solutions)
end

Why the search is small

For a leading digit $b=a^g$, take $a$ primitive: the greatest common divisor of its prime-factor exponents is one. The identity $b^E=N^r$ forces $N=a^h$ for an integer $h$. Enumerate those powers, retain the ones beginning with digit $b$ in the chosen radix, evaluate their suffix, and derive the only possible root order:

\[r=\frac{gE}{h}.\]

A nonintegral result or a root outside the requested domain is rejected. The maximum requested root bounds the relevant suffix by $E\le\lfloor r_{\max}h/g\rfloor$. All larger values share an exact overflow state, so gigantic discarded towers never have to be constructed. Allowed digits, distinct digits, and the 0^0 convention remain configurable.
